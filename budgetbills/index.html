BudgetBills

A money app built around splitting bills, for people who would rather not bring up money.

My role
Solo: research, IA, wireframes, Figma prototype, usability testing
Context
Fundamentals of UX Design, MSc at the University of Edinburgh
When
October to December 2024
Tools
Google Forms, Mural, Balsamiq, Figma

The short version

I set out to build a budgeting app and ended up focused on one moment: asking a friend to pay you back. A survey of 40 people showed that splitting a bill is rarely hard because of the maths. It's hard because someone has to bring it up.

I narrowed the app to four areas, designed and animated a Figma prototype, and ran a task-based test with three people. They completed five of six tasks, but nobody could add a card. Looking back, I also found something the test couldn't show: the bill flow passed every task without solving the problem my research had found.

Research: the problem isn't the maths

I started with four apps people already use, Pocketguard, YNAB, Monzo and Credit Karma, to see what they do well and where they fall short. Then I ran an online survey (40 responses, mostly 18 to 24 and half of them students) and three short recorded interviews.

70%don't use any money app other than their bank's
77.5%want to see their spending in real time, the most-wanted feature
90%want bill splitting built into the way they already pay

People rarely fight about splitting a bill. What they dread is being the one who asks. The cost is social, not mathematical.

The interviews pointed the same way on spending. All three people checked their spending every day and wanted real-time alerts. One asked to see what share of their whole balance was left, not just what they'd spent in a shop.

I turned the survey and interviews into three personas and a journey map, which kept the young, newly independent user in front of me through the rest of the project.

Cutting the scope to four areas

My first information architecture tried to do everything: budgets, transactions, monthly reports, leaderboards, recommendations. Tutor feedback on my first submission was that it was too wide, and with four weeks left to build the prototype, I agreed.

I cut it down to four areas around splitting money: Home, Budget, Bill and Cards. Leaderboards, monthly reports and anything investment-related went.

From sketches to screens

I sketched on paper first, mapped the user flow, then built mid-fidelity wireframes in Balsamiq before moving to Figma.

Design decisions

  • A darker, calmer paletteAll three interviewees preferred deep blues and greens. One said a darker shade of the blue I'd started with (#4F86C6) would suit a finance app better, so I moved to a deep teal.
  • No bottom tab barMoney screens need room for numbers. I swapped the tab bar for a text menu at the top and a summary sheet that slides up from the bottom. Testers were surprised at first, but said it didn't get in their way.
  • A grid of large tilesThe home screen and side menu use a bento-style grid, so everything lives in boxes of the same shape and people learn where things are quickly.
  • Onboarding that shows the featuresFour onboarding screens, one per area: transactions, cards, bill splitting and budgets. An animated logo splash comes first and covers loading time.

The prototype

It was my first time using Figma, and I hadn't known you could animate in it. The splash and onboarding took far longer than planned, but every tester mentioned them. These clips are recorded from the prototype.

Usability test: six tasks, three people

I picked three survey respondents with different levels of experience with money apps: little, some and a lot. Each session ran remotely for about 15 minutes. They shared the Figma prototype on screen and their camera, so I could watch both their cursor and their face. A task only counted as a success if they finished it without a hint.

On looks, people called the app professional, readable and neat. The most useful comment stung a little: readable and neat but no character to show brand. Others said the icons varied in size and there was too much empty space under the summary sheet.

Looking back: where the design fell short

I went back through my own screens with the test results and survey data open beside them. Three problems stood out.

Adding a card: 0 of 3

  1. 1This barcode icon was the only way to add a card. Bank cards don't have barcodes, so the icon gave people nothing to recognise.
  2. 2There was a second route, through the summary sheet on Home, but I hadn't wired it up in the prototype. In the wireframes, Cards had its own tab in a bottom bar; removing the tab bar to free up space hid the path further. One tester suggested a text button, or putting "Add card" under Manage Card.

Splitting: passed the test, missed the point

  1. 1Everyone could send money, so the task passed 3 out of 3. But there's no way to divide one bill between several people, and asking for money is still something you have to do yourself. The flow never touches the problem nearly half my survey described: having to bring it up.

What I'd change next

I haven't designed or tested these changes yet. This is the plan, tied to the evidence behind each change and how I'd check it worked.

A split flow that does the asking for you

  1. Pay with your card as usual.
  2. Tap Split on the transaction itself, so splitting is part of how you pay (90% asked for this).
  3. Pick who was there. BudgetBills divides the amount evenly or by item.
  4. BudgetBills sends the request in its own neutral voice, so nobody has to word an awkward message.
  5. You can see who has paid, and a gentle reminder goes out automatically.

If this were a live product, I'd also watch how many split requests actually get paid, how long that takes, and how often people tap Split on a transaction.

What I learned

  • Passing a task isn't the same as solving the problemUsability tests tell you whether people can use a flow. I now check separately that the flow tackles what the research found.
  • Icons borrow meaning from what people already knowA barcode means scanning a product. Cards don't have barcodes, so the icon couldn't work however well it was drawn.
  • Cutting scope made the work betterFour areas done properly beat ten done thinly.
  • Small samples still show clear signalsThree people is not enough to prove much. But when all three fail at the same step, that's worth fixing first.
