BudgetBills

A personal finance app centred on bill splitting, designed to remove the social friction of asking for money.

Role
Sole product designer and researcher
Responsibilities
User research, information architecture, wireframing, prototyping, usability testing
Timeline
October to December 2024
Tools
Google Forms, Mural, Balsamiq, Figma
01

Overview

BudgetBills began as a broad budgeting app and narrowed to a single moment: asking a friend to pay you back. A survey of 40 respondents showed that splitting a bill is rarely difficult because of the calculation. It's difficult because someone has to raise it.

I reduced the scope to four core areas, designed an animated Figma prototype and ran moderated usability tests with three participants. Most tasks were completed without assistance, but no participant could add a card. A post-test review then exposed a gap the tasks couldn't: the bill flow passed every test without addressing the core problem the research had identified.

02

Discovery research

I began with a competitive analysis of four established finance apps, Pocketguard, YNAB, Monzo and Credit Karma, to map their strengths and gaps. I then ran an online survey (40 respondents; 77.5% aged 18 to 24, 52.5% students), followed by three interviews with survey respondents, each given an information sheet and consent form.

47.5%cited talking about money as a challenge when splitting bills
20%reported regular disputes when splitting bills

Users rarely disagree over a split bill; the friction lies in having to ask. The barrier is social, not mathematical.

70%of respondents use no finance app beyond their bank's own
77.5%want real-time expense tracking, the most requested feature
90%want bill splitting integrated with their existing payment method

From data to people

The interviews reinforced the demand for visibility. All three participants reviewed their spending daily and wanted real-time notifications. One asked to see the percentage of their total balance remaining, rather than individual transaction amounts.

I synthesised the survey and interview data into three personas and a user journey map, keeping the primary audience, students and young adults newly managing their own finances, at the centre of later design decisions.

03

Defining the scope

The initial information architecture tried to cover everything: budgets, transactions, monthly reports, leaderboards and recommendations. Tutor feedback on the first submission identified the scope as too broad, and with four weeks remaining for the prototype, I agreed.

I restructured the app around four core areas centred on bill splitting: Home, Budget, Bill and Cards. Leaderboards, monthly reports and investment features were removed from scope.

05

Design decisions

  • A darker, more trustworthy paletteAll three interviewees preferred deep blues and greens, and one suggested a darker shade of the initial blue (#4F86C6) would better suit a finance app. I moved to a deep teal.
  • No bottom navigation barFinancial screens need space for data, so I replaced the tab bar with a top text menu and a summary sheet that slides up from the bottom. Participants were initially surprised but reported no negative impact on their experience.
  • Bento grid layoutThe home screen and side menu use a bento-style grid of consistent tiles, helping users learn the layout quickly.
  • Feature-led onboardingFour onboarding screens introduce each core area: transactions, cards, bill splitting and budgets. An animated logo splash precedes them and masks loading time.
06

High-fidelity prototype

This was my first project in Figma, including its animation features. The splash and onboarding sequences took longer than planned, but the splash screen was praised repeatedly in testing for being simple yet polished.

Usability testing

I recruited three survey respondents with low, moderate and high experience of finance apps. Each moderated remote session lasted around 15 minutes, with participants sharing both their screen and camera so I could observe interactions and reactions. A task counted as successful only if completed without assistance.

08

Design review: where v1 fell short

After testing, I reviewed each screen against the test results and survey data. Three issues stood out.

1 2

Adding a card: 0/3 success

  1. 1A barcode-scan icon was the only visible entry point for adding a card. Bank cards don't carry barcodes, so the icon had no meaning users could map to the task.
  2. 2A second route, via the Home summary sheet, wasn't connected in the prototype. In the wireframes, Cards had a dedicated tab in the bottom bar; removing the bar for screen space buried the path further. One participant suggested a text label, or placing "Add card" under Manage Card.
1 2 3

Home: the most requested feature is hidden

  1. 1The most prominent area is given to a credit score card that isn't yet functional.
  2. 2Real-time spending, requested by 77.5% of respondents, is hidden in a collapsed sheet at the bottom.
  3. 3The shortcut carousel is truncated and leads with Chat; one participant argued My Activity should come first.
1

Bill splitting: passed the test, missed the problem

  1. 1All participants completed the send-funds task (3/3). However, the flow can't divide one bill among several people, and the user still has to initiate every request. It doesn't address the core problem cited by nearly half of respondents: having to raise the subject of money.
09

Next iteration

These changes haven't yet been designed or tested. Each is tied to the evidence behind it and a metric to validate it.

A split flow that asks on the user's behalf

  1. The user pays by card as usual.
  2. They tap Split on the transaction, so splitting is built into the payment flow (requested by 90% of respondents).
  3. They select who was there, and BudgetBills divides the amount evenly or by item.
  4. BudgetBills sends the request in a neutral, app-authored message, so no one has to word an awkward ask.
  5. Payment status is tracked, and polite reminders are sent automatically.

10

Key learnings

  • Task success doesn't equal problem solvedUsability testing shows whether users can complete a flow. I now separately verify that each flow addresses the problem the research identified.
  • Icons rely on users' existing mental modelsA barcode signals scanning a product. Bank cards don't carry barcodes, so the icon couldn't communicate its function, however well it was drawn.
  • Focused scope improves qualityFour core areas executed well delivered more than a broad feature set done thinly.
  • Small samples can still surface clear signalsThree participants can't validate a design, but when all three fail at the same step, it's the clear first priority.
