Thryve

A health app for people who don't track anything, built for real in React Native.

My role
Solo: research, brand, UX/UI, 3D modelling, development
Context
MSc final project and thesis, University of Edinburgh
When
2025
Tools
Google Forms, Figma, Balsamiq, React Native, Appwrite, After Effects, Blender

The short version

Most health apps are built for people who are already motivated. My survey showed that most people don't see themselves as trackers, and many of them don't use any health tool at all. Thryve turns small, everyday health actions into visible progress, with badges and an avatar that grow as you do.

I wrote a manifesto before designing anything, then built the app in React Native so it would behave like a real product, with sign-up, log-in and animation. Feedback on my first badges showed they contradicted my own manifesto, so I redesigned them as robots I sculpted in Blender. Reviewing the finished build, I also found that its core loop never made it onto the home screen.

Health apps reward people who are already motivated

People mainly quit health apps because a feature they need is missing or nothing keeps them going (Mustafa et al., 2022). The apps I studied each lean towards people who are already committed:

  • StravaBuilt around competition: segments, leaderboards, personal bests.
  • FitbitThe best features depend on owning the device.
  • Pokémon GoGets people walking, but health is a side effect of the game, not the goal.

I surveyed 30 people about their habits and what keeps them going. 70% were 18 to 24, and 60% described themselves as moderately active.

22 of 30didn't see themselves as people who love tracking progress and setting goals
9 of those 22used no health tool at all, compared with 1 of the 8 trackers
50%picked streaks, and 50% badges, as the most motivating features

People who aren't very motivated don't need more data. They need a small goal they can hit today and a result they can see.

Among the 22 non-trackers, the most requested support was personalised goals and habit tracking (11 people), then daily motivation (9) and visual progress (9).

Principles before pixels

When the idea was still loose, my supervisor suggested writing brand guidelines first. The manifesto settled the biggest question: Thryve is an app with game elements, not a game. It set three rules that I later used to judge my own work:

  • Progress without shame, growth without guilt. Missing a day is never punished.
  • Every action counts, even a glass of water.
  • Play for progression, not perfection. It's play, not competition.

The structure came from the Korean webtoon Solo Leveling, where the hero gets stronger by completing small daily quests. I paired it with motivation research, mainly self-determination theory and the difference between playful and goal-driven game design.

Building it for real

I mapped the flow and wireframed the app in Balsamiq, then chose to build it rather than stop at a Figma prototype. Prototypes can't take real text input, so they always feel like a demo. Swift needs a Mac, which I didn't have, so I picked React Native and learned it during the project.

  • Real accountsSign-up and log-in connect to an Appwrite database, so new users are created and returning users keep their data.
  • Questions from the surveyThe first question offers the survey's top three reasons for staying healthy: looks (70%), energy (60%) and feeling good mentally (57%).
  • Motion from my own toolsI animated the splash screen in After Effects and dropped it into the build as video. Onboarding animations use Lottie files.
  • Problems along the wayExpo Go couldn't preview 3D assets on a phone. The newer video package couldn't hide its playback controls, so I went back to the older one. The onboarding slider's Next button was unreliable, so I removed the progress dots.

Badges: users caught what I missed

My first badges were emblems with wings and sigils. In the first round of feedback, people said they looked militaristic. They were right: that clashed directly with a manifesto about play and inclusion.

I sculpted a small robot in Blender and rebuilt the set around it. The robot grows up as you level up, and the shape behind it gains a corner at each level, so progress shows without a single number:

The robots went down well in the second round of feedback. But people pointed out that the first-login and seven-day badges looked like they belonged to a different app, so I brought them into the same visual language.

Trading real-time 3D for video

The avatar went the same way as the badges: my first realistic human model didn't fit the playful tone, so I moved to a stylised humanoid. I wanted users to turn and play with it in the app, but React Native only loads .glb files directly, and that format drops the textures and modifiers I'd applied. The libraries that would have fixed this needed more time than I had. So I rendered the avatars as video and embedded them in the build. They look as intended and work on both platforms, but you can't interact with them.

Looking back at the build

Both feedback rounds were about how the badges looked. Nobody tried to complete a task with the app, so I missed problems in how it works. Going back through the finished build, I found these:

Home: nothing to do today

  2. 2The space goes to four menu tiles. The core loop, do a small task and watch your robot grow, isn't on the first screen at all.

  3. 3Rest is already at Level 5, and nothing explains how to level up. The feature meant to make growth visible makes it confusing. The tab that would have let people upgrade their avatar was also left unfinished.

Achievements: one badge at a time

  1. 1Each badge gets a full screen, so you can't see how many you have, what's left, or what to aim for next. Half the survey named badges as the most motivating feature, and this view doesn't use that.

The data also challenged one of my own bets. I made the avatar central, but custom avatars came last in the survey (13%). I meant it as a way of showing progress rather than dressing up, but the risk was real. Next time I'd lead with daily quests and make the avatar support them.

What I'd change next

These are directions, not finished or tested designs. I'd tackle them in this order, because the home screen carries the core loop.

ScreenChangeHow I'd measure it
HomeThree quests for today at the top, based on the first-question answers. One tap to complete, then the robot reacts and XP goes up. After a missed day: "Starting again is fine."Time to finish a first quest, and how many manage it
ProfileOne XP number everywhere, with a bar showing XP to the next level. Categories become secondary.Can people explain their own level correctly?
AchievementsA grid of every badge, with locked ones showing what it takes to earn them.Can people find what the next badge needs?
QuestionsA step counter (1 of 2), a smaller decorative ring, and a preview of the quests the answer unlocks.Completion rate of the questions

In a live product I'd also track seven-day return rate and quests completed per day.

What I learned

  • Users spot it when the work drifts from the principlesI wrote "play, not competition", then designed military-looking badges. Feedback caught it straight away.
  • Asking for opinions isn't usability testingImpressions told me how the badges looked, not whether the app worked. You have to give people tasks and watch. That's what I did on BudgetBills, and it's what Thryve was missing.
  • Put the core loop on the first screenMore features don't help if the main thing you want people to do isn't where they land.
  • Building it changes the design conversationCoding the app showed me constraints, like the .glb limit, that a prototype would have hidden. I now think about how a design will be built while I'm still designing it.
